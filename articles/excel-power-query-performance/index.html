The Third Stage of Data Processing / Automation

In one of my previous projects, I worked with a 44 MB Excel dataset containing one year of operational data. Through continuous experimentation and improvement, I eventually developed an approach that could process the required data in approximately 10 seconds.

From my experience, business data is commonly organized in two ways: time-based data, such as monthly reports covering up to 31 days, and item-based data, such as transactions, products, or other categories, depending on the requirements.

Initially, I retrieved data directly from the raw sheets (Masterlist), using Excel formulas with ranges extending up to 15,000 rows. Although this approach worked, Excel had to repeatedly evaluate large ranges, even when only a small portion of the data was required. This approach would become impractical if the dataset grew tenfold (200,000 rows).

To reduce unnecessary calculations, I introduced a helper sheet, as using Name Manager alone did not resolve the issue. Instead of having every formula refer directly to the Masterlist, I used FILTER() to extract only the relevant records into the helper sheet. The remaining formulas could then process this smaller dataset.

However, even with the help of FILTER(), the process was still relatively slow and resource-intensive. Some operations could take up to 30 seconds, with noticeable CPU usage and heat generation.

Towards the end of my second year, I discovered that Power Query M worked much better for my scenario. Instead of relying entirely on worksheet formulas, Power Query allows us to extract, filter, and transform data through its query engine before loading the required results into Excel. By using the Advanced Editor, I could define which columns to extract, apply the required transformations, and load the processed results directly into the helper sheet. This reduced unnecessary worksheet calculations and improved the overall processing performance.

However, I encountered another problem. After refreshing the Power Query output, some formulas linked to the helper sheet no longer maintained their references. After experimenting with different approaches, I found that using INDIRECT() in the affected formulas helped maintain the references in my workbook.

With these changes, I eventually achieved a processing time of approximately 10 seconds for the required data (8 seconds for the Power Query M refresh and 2 seconds for formula calculations).

Although Excel is a powerful tool, it has its limitations, particularly when dealing with increasingly large datasets. A single worksheet supports a maximum of 1,048,576 rows.

The next step would be the final stage: developing a system capable of managing billions to trillions of records. At that scale, Excel alone would no longer be practical. Learning to develop databases, processing engines, servers, and scalable data architectures becomes essential.
